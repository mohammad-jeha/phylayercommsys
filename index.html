L0 · Foundations

What the physical layer actually does

Layer 1 moves raw bits across the air. Everything above it (MAC scheduling, RLC, PDCP, IP) assumes the PHY delivers transport blocks at some rate and error probability. PHY research is about pushing that rate, reliability, latency, energy and now sensing accuracy toward physical limits.

In 5G NR the physical layer is specified in TS 38.211 (physical channels and modulation), 38.212 (multiplexing and channel coding), 38.213/38.214 (control and data procedures) and 38.215 (measurements).

Two boundaries are worth stating precisely: HARQ is a MAC procedure, but soft combining of retransmissions happens in the PHY decoder; beam management spans PHY measurements (SSB, CSI-RS) and higher-layer control.

L0 · Foundations

Signals, noise and the Shannon limit

Wireless signals are represented at complex baseband: an in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) component per sample. Noise is modelled as additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN). The single most important formula in the field bounds what any scheme can achieve.

C = B log2(1 + SNR)   bit/s(2.1) capacity of the AWGN channel
Eb/N0 = SNR · B/R    ⇒   Eb/N0 ≥ (2η − 1)/η(2.2) η = R/B, spectral efficiency
limη→0 (2η − 1)/η = ln 2 ≈ −1.59 dB(2.3) the ultimate Shannon limit
N = −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log10B + NF(2.4) thermal noise at 290 K

dB fluency

+3 dB = ×2, +10 dB = ×10, +20 dB = ×100. dBm is power relative to 1 mW: 30 dBm = 1 W, 46 dBm ≈ 40 W. Gains and losses add in dB; this is why link budgets are written as sums.

SNR, SINR, Es/N0, Eb/N0

SNR is per-sample power ratio in the signal bandwidth. SINR adds interference to the denominator. Es/N0 is energy per symbol; Eb/N0 = Es/N0 divided by bits per symbol (and code rate). Use Eb/N0 to compare schemes fairly.

Sampling and baseband

A complex baseband signal of bandwidth B needs ≥ B complex samples/s (Nyquist). A passband carrier at fc is recovered by I/Q down-conversion; the PHY math happens almost entirely at baseband.

L1 · Core link

Modulation, detection and hardware reality

Bits are Gray-mapped onto constellation points so neighbouring points differ by one bit. The optimal receiver in AWGN picks the nearest point (maximum likelihood = minimum Euclidean distance); modern receivers pass soft log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) to the decoder instead of hard decisions.

Modulation

Theory curves: BPSK Q(√(2Es/N0)); QPSK 2Q(√γ) − Q²(√γ); M-PSK ≈ 2Q(√(2γ) sin(π/M)); square M-QAM 1 − (1 − 2(1 − 1/√M) Q(√(3γ/(M − 1))))². 3GPP TS 38.104 caps base-station EVM at 17.5 % (QPSK), 12.5 % (16-QAM), 8 % (64-QAM), 3.5 % (256-QAM) and 2.5 % (1024-QAM).

PAPR

OFDM sums many subcarriers, so the peak-to-average power ratio is high (often 10–12 dB at 10⁻⁴ CCDF). Power amplifiers must back off, which costs efficiency. That is why the 5G and 6G uplink keep DFT-spread OFDM as an option.

Phase noise

Oscillator jitter rotates the whole constellation. It grows roughly 20 dB per decade of carrier frequency, which is why mmWave NR added PT-RS (phase-tracking reference signals) and why sub-THz waveform design is still open.

Probabilistic shaping

Uniform QAM leaves up to 1.53 dB (the shaping gain) on the table at high SNR. Probabilistic constellation shaping, standard in optical links, is studied as a 6G modulation enhancement.

L1 · Core link

The wireless channel

Three scales matter. Path loss (distance, frequency, blockage) sets the mean; shadowing (log-normal, a few dB) varies over tens of metres; multipath fading varies over fractions of a wavelength. Delay spread causes frequency selectivity; Doppler spread causes time selectivity.

L2 · Systems

OFDM and 5G NR numerology

OFDM turns one frequency-selective channel into many parallel flat ones via the FFT. With a cyclic prefix (CP) longer than the channel's delay spread, linear convolution becomes circular, so each subcarrier sees a single complex gain: Yk = HkXk + Wk. That one-tap equaliser is why OFDM underpins 4G, 5G, Wi-Fi and, by 3GPP's 2025 decision, the 6G downlink.

6G starting point

At RAN#109 (Sept 2025) 3GPP took these subcarrier spacings as the starting point for 6GR: 15 kHz for sub-6 GHz FDD, 30 kHz for sub-6 GHz TDD, 30 or 60 kHz around 7 GHz, and 120 kHz for 24.25–52.6 GHz. The 5G slot-based frame and scalable numerology are reused. System bandwidth spans from 3 MHz in some spectrum up to 400 MHz (the 7 GHz range), and a low-tier IoT device class with a narrow maximum bandwidth is being defined from the start.

Costs of OFDM

High PAPR; sensitivity to carrier-frequency offset and phase noise (inter-carrier interference); CP overhead (~7 %); out-of-band emission that needs windowing or filtering; performance loss under very high Doppler.

DFT-s-OFDM

A DFT precoder before the IFFT makes the signal single-carrier-like with lower PAPR. Kept for the 6G uplink, and now extended to multi-layer (up to two-layer) UL MIMO.

Reference signals

SSB (sync + broadcast, 4 symbols × 240 subcarriers), DMRS (demodulation), CSI-RS (downlink CSI and beams), SRS (uplink sounding, TDD reciprocity), PT-RS (phase tracking), PRS (positioning).

L2 · Systems

Channel coding, from Hamming to 6G

Hamming and convolutional codes (Viterbi decoding) → turbo codes (3G/4G) → LDPC and polar codes (5G), each step closing the gap to Shannon with practical decoders.

LDPC for data

Sparse parity-check matrices decoded by belief propagation, highly parallel for multi-Gbps throughput. NR uses base graph 1 (large blocks, up to 8448 info bits, rates down to 1/3) and base graph 2 (small blocks, rates down to 1/5), with lifting sizes and incremental-redundancy HARQ.

Polar for control

Arıkan's channel polarisation (2009), the first provably capacity-achieving construction with low complexity. NR uses CRC-aided successive-cancellation list (CA-SCL) decoding for DCI, UCI and PBCH.

6G decision so far

RAN1 agreed to largely reuse 5G coding: LDPC for data with enhancements under discussion (including an additional base graph for decoder area efficiency) and polar for control. Research value lies in decoders (energy per bit at 100+ Gbps), short blocks, and joint coding–modulation.

L2 · Systems

MIMO, massive MIMO and beamforming

With Nt transmit and Nr receive antennas, y = Hx + n. Antennas buy three things: array gain (SNR ×N), diversity (steeper error curves) and multiplexing (parallel streams, capacity slope min(Nt, Nr)). Zheng and Tse's diversity–multiplexing trade-off (2003) says you cannot max out both.

C = E[ log2 det( I + (ρ/Nt) HHH ) ](7.1) ergodic capacity, no CSIT
WMRT ∝ HH,   WZF ∝ HH(HHH)−1,   WRZF ∝ HH(HHH + (K/ρ)I)−1(7.2) linear precoders

Massive MIMO (Marzetta, 2010)

With M ≫ K antennas, channels harden (gain concentrates at its mean) and users become nearly orthogonal (favourable propagation), so simple MRT/ZF approach optimal. TDD reciprocity lets uplink pilots give downlink CSI; pilot contamination from reused pilots is the classic limit.

Hybrid beamforming

At mmWave, one RF chain per antenna is too costly, so an analog phase-shifter network feeds fewer digital chains. Newer ideas: fully digital arrays with low-resolution ADCs, and "tri-hybrid" designs that add reconfigurable antennas as a third layer.

Beam management in NR

P1 (SSB sweep, coarse), P2 (CSI-RS refinement at gNB), P3 (UE receive beam). Codebooks: Type I (single/multi-panel) and Type II (high-resolution, linear combination of beams). AI-based beam prediction is normative in Rel-19.

L3 · 6G & standards

What 3GPP has already decided for the 6G radio

  • WaveformDownlink CP-OFDM. Uplink supports CP-OFDM and DFT-s-OFDM, with DFT-s-OFDM extended to UL MIMO (RAN1 agreements, Aug–Oct 2025). OTFS/AFDM are not the baseline.
  • ModulationUniform QAM as the baseline; enhancements such as shaping remain under study.
  • Coding5G channel codes largely reused (LDPC data, polar control), with targeted enhancements such as an additional LDPC base graph under discussion.
  • Frame & numerology5G slot-based structure and scalable numerology reused; longer reference-signal periodicities studied so base stations can switch off hardware (energy saving).
  • BandwidthFrom 3 MHz in some spectrum to 400 MHz channels in the 7 GHz range (vs 100 MHz typical 5G mid-band).
  • DevicesDesigned from day one for smartphones through low-tier massive-IoT devices (single-antenna minimum for the lowest tier).
  • SensingSix sensing modes under study: gNB monostatic, gNB–gNB bistatic, gNB→UE, UE→gNB, UE–UE bistatic and UE monostatic. Rel-19 already produced an ISAC channel model.
  • EnergyEnergy efficiency is a quantitative KPI for both network and device, evaluated at empty and partial load.
  • CoexistenceMulti-RAT spectrum sharing (MRSS) with 5G NR, NTN support, multi-TRP, open interfaces.

L4 · Group research map

Where this atlas meets Dr. Chaaban's research

Dr. Anas Chaaban (Associate Professor, School of Engineering, UBC Okanagan) applies information theory, signal processing and machine learning to wireless and optical communication. His group's 2022–2026 papers fall into five lines. Each card summarises the line, lists representative papers, and points to the parts of this atlas that build the background for it.

Wave-domain processing

Stacked intelligent surfaces

A stacked intelligent surface (SIS, also called SIM) cascades several programmable metasurface layers in front of a few RF chains, so beamforming, channel estimation and even equalisation happen as the wave propagates. The group has shown a hybrid digital–wave-domain channel estimator whose error approaches that of a 64-RF-chain digital estimator with only 4 RF chains; modelled the SIS as a complex-valued neural network for end-to-end optimisation; proposed nonlinear SIS whose step-like unit cells behave like activation functions, and delay-augmented SIS that can equalise multipath in analog; and applied SIS to MIMO ISAC and to the training–throughput trade-off of multi-user MISO.

From unit cell to system

Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces

The RIS work spans hardware and systems. With electromagnetics co-author Loïc Markley, the group designs unit cells whose reflection phase and phase–frequency slope can be tuned independently (a 2.5 GHz varactor-loaded design gives about 320° of phase range) and optimises that slope so a surface stays effective across a wideband OFDM carrier. System studies cover multi-RIS cell-free massive MIMO, aerial and ground users, RIS-aided index modulation, RIS-induced channel randomness for secret-key generation, passive Wi-Fi localisation and mmWave RIS hardware.

Antenna-domain beamforming

Reconfigurable antennas and tri-hybrid beamforming

Tri-hybrid beamforming adds the antenna's own radiation pattern as a third precoding stage after digital and analog. The group models electromagnetically reconfigurable antennas with spherical-harmonic pattern expansions and optimises patterns, phases and digital precoders jointly with WMMSE and Riemannian-manifold methods. Their TCOM study separates a hardware-constrained model from a performance-bound model: the potential gains are large, but current antenna hardware cannot yet realise most of them. A 2026 follow-up unfolds the optimiser into a trainable graph neural network for dynamic metasurface antennas, matching sum-rate with over ten times lower runtime.

Information theory for light

Optical wireless and IM/DD channels

Optical wireless links use intensity modulation with direct detection (IM/DD): the input is a non-negative light intensity with peak and average limits, so Gaussian inputs are infeasible and capacity has no closed form. The group derives computable capacity bounds for the two-user IM/DD interference channel (with generalised degrees of freedom), shows that evenly spaced discrete inputs give tight inner bounds for the peak-constrained broadcast channel, builds capacity-preserving binary channel models so binary codes approach capacity, and studies precoding for optical MISO broadcasting from an information-theoretic view. Related work covers optical RISs for covert VLC and ultraviolet non-line-of-sight localisation.

    From virtualised network functions to physical-layer functions

    My master's research focused on distributed, explainable and predictive orchestration of virtual network functions for secure 5G/6G edge networks.     The group's 45-page survey From Centralized RAN to Open RAN: A Survey on the Evolution of Distributed Antenna Systems (2024) traces this path through C-RAN, fog RAN, virtualised RAN and cell-free massive MIMO to O-RAN.     In one breath

    "In my master's I learned to place, predict and secure functions under latency and compute constraints. In a virtualised RAN the physical layer is the heaviest of those functions, and 6G wants it AI-native. I want to move down the stack and work on the signal processing and information theory themselves, while keeping a systems view of what is implementable."

    L4 · Research frontier

    Three proposed research directions

    Each direction extends a line of the group's recent work (see the research map) and uses the background built in this atlas. They are starting points for discussion, and I would shape any of them around the group's ongoing projects.

    Direction A · Stacked intelligent surfaces × ISAC

    Training-aware stacked intelligent surfaces for joint sensing and communication

    When one stacked intelligent surface must serve users and illuminate sensing targets with only a few RF chains, how should its layers, phases and training overhead be designed to reach the best rate–sensing-accuracy trade-off?

    Builds on
    SIS-assisted MIMO ISAC (IEEE Network, 2025); the training–throughput trade-off in SIM-assisted multi-user MISO (IEEE TCOM, 2026); hybrid digital–wave-domain channel estimation (IEEE WCNC, 2023); complex-valued neural-network modelling of SIS (IEEE MeditCom, 2025).
    Why now
    ISAC is an IMT-2030 usage scenario and 3GPP is studying six sensing modes for 6G. Wave-domain processing promises large apertures with few RF chains, but sensing accuracy, estimation overhead and multi-user rate have not been optimised together.
    Method
    Layer-by-layer Rayleigh–Sommerfeld model (Lab 12); Cramér–Rao bound for target angle observed through the surface; training overhead charged against throughput; end-to-end optimisation using the complex-valued network model; unit-cell responses constrained to realistic phase and phase–frequency behaviour.
    Year-1 output
    Reproduce an SIS-ISAC baseline; derive the CRB–rate region including training; one conference paper and a journal draft.

    Direction B · Reconfigurable antennas

    Closing the gap in tri-hybrid beamforming: realisable pattern codebooks

    Which small set of physically realisable radiation patterns captures most of the ideal tri-hybrid gain, and can that set be learned jointly with the digital and analog precoders at real-time complexity?

    Builds on
    Tri-hybrid precoding with pattern-reconfigurable antennas (IEEE TCOM, 2026) and with electromagnetically reconfigurable antennas (IEEE Globecom Workshops, 2025); beampattern synthesis with ERAs (EUSIPCO, 2025); fast tri-hybrid beamforming via deep unfolding (2026).
    Why now
    The group's results show large gains from optimising radiation patterns in the ideal model, much of which disappears after projection onto what current antennas can realise. The open question is which patterns the hardware should provide.
    Method
    Spherical-harmonic pattern models; codebook learning with projection onto realisable sets inside the training loop; WMMSE and Riemannian baselines; deep unfolding for speed; extensions to near-field users and sensing beampatterns (Labs 7, 9 and 13).
    Year-1 output
    A benchmark of realisable pattern sets against the performance bound, and a codebook-design paper with open code.

    Direction C · Optical wireless × learning

    Learned coding for peak- and average-limited optical intensity channels

    How close can learned coding and modulation, trained under non-negativity, peak and average constraints, get to the known capacity bounds of IM/DD channels, including MISO broadcasting, at short and moderate blocklengths?

    Builds on
    Binary modelling and capacity-approaching coding for IM/DD (IEEE TCOM, 2023); evenly spaced discrete inputs for the IM/DD broadcast channel (IEEE Globecom, 2022); precoding for optical MISO broadcasting (IEEE JSAC, 2025); CNN and multi-level autoencoders for coding (2023, 2025).
    Why now
    Optical wireless offers licence-free bandwidth for indoor 6G, and learned codes have become competitive at short blocklengths in several studies. Information-theoretic bounds give an exact yardstick for judging them.
    Method
    Autoencoders whose output layer enforces 0 ≤ x ≤ A and an average-intensity budget; comparison with capacity bounds (Lab 14) and finite-blocklength limits (Lab 6); extension to multi-user MISO broadcasting with the group's precoding results as baselines.
    Year-1 output
    A learned-versus-bound benchmark for IM/DD channels and a conference paper, with optional validation on a VLC link.

    Also a natural fit

    Two further lines connect directly to my work on secure, distributed edge networks: RIS-induced channel randomness for secret-key generation, and distributed antenna systems on the path from C-RAN to Open RAN.
